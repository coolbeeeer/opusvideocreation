## Accuracy notes
Each line, what it claims, and what the picture simplifies.

1. **"It depends on how big it is."** NASA: black holes are grouped by mass into stellar-mass (a few to hundreds of
   Suns), supermassive (hundreds of thousands to billions of Suns) and intermediate ones
   ([NASA, types of black holes](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/types/)). Size means mass here: a
   black hole's horizon grows in proportion to its mass (about 3 km of radius per solar mass).
2. **"A small one, ten times as heavy as the Sun, pulls much harder on your feet than on your head."** Gravity
   weakens with distance, so the end of you nearer the hole is pulled harder: the tidal force. For 10 Suns the horizon
   is about 30 km in radius. The difference in pull between the head and feet of a 1.8 m person is about 100 times
   Earth's gravity some 1,700 km from the hole, and about 19 million times at the horizon (my calculation, Newtonian
   approximation: 2GML/r³).
3. **"You'd be stretched into a noodle before you even reach it. Astronomers really call this spaghettification."**
   NASA: stellar-mass black holes "can rip apart approaching objects before they get to the horizon", and
   "astrophysicists call" the stretching spaghettification
   ([NASA, 2024](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/new-nasa-black-hole-visualization-takes-viewers-beyond-the-brink/)).
   The noodle is drawn straight because a body falling straight in is stretched along the line to the centre; the
   swirling spirals in many pictures belong to gas that orbits.
4. **"A giant one, like the one at the center of our galaxy, is gentler. You'd cross its edge, the event horizon,
   and feel nothing."** The Milky Way's central black hole, Sagittarius A*, has about 4 million times the Sun's mass
   ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/types/); NASA's 2024 simulation uses 4.3 million) and a
   horizon about 12.5 million km in radius ([NASA SVS](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/14576)). NASA: "If you have the
   choice, you want to fall into a supermassive black hole." At its horizon the head-to-feet difference for a person is
   about a ten-thousandth of Earth's gravity (same calculation as above), so nothing is felt.
5. **"A friend watching from far away sees you slow down, turn red and fade, as if frozen at the edge."** To a distant
   observer the infalling camera's image "would slow and then seem to freeze just shy of" the horizon (NASA, 2024);
   the observer sees the object "redden and fade out of sight, but they will never see it cross the event horizon"
   ([Astronomy](https://www.astronomy.com/science/ask-astro-can-an-observer-ever-see-something-fall-into-a-black-hole/)).
   The image does not stay forever: it reddens and dims out of view, so "frozen" is how it seems, which is why the
   line says "as if".
6. **"For you, time goes on. Inside, every path leads to the center, and you have only seconds left."** Once inside,
   "both the camera and the space-time in which it's moving rush toward the black hole's center", and in NASA's
   simulation "its destruction by spaghettification is just 12.8 seconds away" after crossing (NASA, 2024). The
   countdown's 0:12 uses that figure. The time depends on the path: about 28 s for a straight fall from far away into
   Sagittarius A*, and at most about a minute for any path (my calculation, non-rotating black hole: (4/3)GM/c³ and
   πGM/c³). Even the giant one spaghettifies you in the end, very near the centre (about 128,000 km from it, NASA SVS).
7. **"What happens there? Nobody knows. Our laws of physics break down."** At the singularity "the laws of physics as
   we know them cease to operate" (NASA, 2024). Specifically, general relativity predicts infinite density there, and a
   theory of quantum gravity that would say what really happens does not exist yet. The three equations stand for
   "laws of physics" in general; the one that fails is general relativity.
8. **"So if you get the choice, pick a giant one. Or better: be the friend."** The first half echoes NASA's Jeremy
   Schnittman (quoted above); the second is the film's joke.

Picture simplifications:
- Not to scale. The astronaut is drawn about as big as the small black hole; a real 10-Sun horizon is some 60 km
  across. The giant one is drawn only a few times bigger than the small one; Sagittarius A* is about 400,000 times
  wider.
- The black holes do not spin, as in NASA's simulation. Real ones spin, and inside a spinning black hole the
  structure is more complicated and less certain.
- The black disk is drawn as the horizon itself. The dark "shadow" an outside observer sees is about 2.6 times wider
  than the horizon, and the glowing disk and arcs are stylised versions of the lensed images in NASA's simulation.
- Inside the horizon the film shows dark space with light streaming in. A falling observer would see a distorted
  view of the outside sky (light keeps falling in behind them), not a lit tunnel, and the bright "centre" is a
  symbol: the singularity cannot be seen.
- The arrows show the pull on the feet and the head; their lengths are not to scale either (see note 2).
